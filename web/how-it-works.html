How it works

8ball plans the wiring of a building's alarm and sensor system. Give it a floor plan and it decides how many control panels to use, where to put them, and exactly where every cable runs. This page explains the problem, the rules, how we solve it and how well it works. You don't need any background.

The problem in plain words

Devices. Picture a flat or an office floor. Every living room, bedroom, kitchen and storage room needs one small device on its ceiling, such as a smoke detector or a sensor. Bathrooms, halls and balconies get none.

Hubs. Each device must be wired to a control panel, which we call a hub: a small box on a wall that listens to all the devices it looks after. A hub can only look after so much: at most 5 devices and at most 100 m² of floor. A big flat therefore needs several hubs.

Loops. The cable does not run out to each device separately. It leaves the hub, visits every device of its group in turn and comes back to the hub: one closed loop. If any single cable is cut or damaged, every device can still be reached from the other side of the loop.

The job. For each floor plan, decide how many hubs to use, where each one sits, which rooms each one looks after, in what order its loop visits the devices, and the exact path of every cable. The answer with the fewest hubs wins. Among answers with the same number of hubs, the one with the cheapest cabling wins.

Four words to know

These four words are used everywhere in the app and on this page.

Device

One sensor in one room

Every living room, bedroom, kitchen and storage room gets exactly one. In our files and code a device is called a node.

Hub

The control panel

It sits inside one of the rooms it looks after. Its cable loop starts and ends at the hub.

Zone

The rooms one hub looks after

Every room that needs a device belongs to exactly one zone, and every zone has exactly one hub.

Loop

The closed cable path of a zone

Hub, then every device of the zone once, then back to the hub. One loop per hub.

The rules

These are the hard rules. An answer that breaks any one of them scores zero for that plan, so 8ball never produces one and an independent checker looks for every single one (step 10 below).

Size

At most 100 m² per zone

The floor areas of a zone's rooms add up to no more than 100 m².

Devices

At most 5 devices per zone

Because every room has one device, that is also at most 5 rooms in a zone.

Connected

A zone's rooms must be joined

You must be able to walk between all of a zone's rooms through doors or open passages (a gap in a wall at least 0.6 m wide) without leaving the zone. Passing through a bathroom, stair or balcony does not count.

Cable path

Straight lines and right angles

Cable runs only horizontally or vertically. It stays inside the building outline, keeps about 4 cm away from walls and windows, and changes rooms only through a door or an open passage.

Own zone

Never into another hub's rooms

A loop stays in its own zone's rooms. It may cross rooms that have no device, such as a hall or a bathroom.

Hub spot

Away from walls

The hub sits inside a room of its zone, at least 0.5 m from every wall, and never on top of a device.

How answers are ranked

Two answers to the same plan are compared in two steps. The second step only matters when the first is a tie.

1

Fewest hubs

An answer with fewer hubs always beats one with more hubs, however cheap its cable. Fewer hubs means fewer boxes to buy, mount and maintain.

2

Then the lowest cost J

If the number of hubs is the same, the answer with the lower total cost J wins. Lower is better.

What goes into the cost J

J = L + 5X + O + 0.5B
L · length

Metres of cable

Every metre of cable costs 1 point. Shorter loops are cheaper.

X · crossings

5 points each

Counted wherever one cable crosses or touches another cable, or runs through another loop's device. They are risky and hard to install.

O · overlap

Metres on the same line

Counted where two cables run along exactly the same line, within 1 mm. 1 point per metre.

B · bends

0.5 points each

Every 90° corner in the cable. Fewer corners are easier to install.

Example. A loop with 40 m of cable, no crossings, no overlap and 12 bends costs J = 40 + 5×0 + 0 + 0.5×12 = 46.

How 8ball finds the answer

Ten steps, from a raw drawing to a checked answer. Each step says what happens in plain words, then names the technique and the details for readers who want them.

Read the plan

A floor plan is a set of shapes: rooms, walls, doors, windows and the front door. 8ball reads them from the ResPlan dataset (17,000 flats) or from a file you upload, and converts the drawing's units to metres.

Polygon geometryShapely

  • The drawing's units are not metres, so the scale is worked out from the flat's stated area: scale = √(inner area ÷ stated area).
  • Broken polygons are repaired. Every shape of at least 0.5 m² of an eligible type (living room, bedroom, kitchen, storage) becomes a room with one device. Smaller slivers are dropped and logged.
  • Until the organisers give a device format, devices are generated in two ways for testing: in the middle of the room (the deepest point, at least 0.5 m from the walls) or at a seeded random spot.

Work out where cable may go, and which rooms connect

8ball marks the floor where a cable may run: inside rooms, clear of walls and windows. It also finds which rooms each door or open passage joins, because zones must be connected through them.

Free-floor maskDoor and passage graph

  • Free floor = the building outline minus walls and windows, shrunk by 4 cm.
  • A door joins every room that covers at least 5% of its surroundings or touches it along at least 25% of its longest contact. The front door joins nothing.
  • An open passage is a stretch of shared room boundary at least 0.6 m long that has no wall, door or window.
  • The result is a graph: rooms are points, doors and passages are links. A zone has to be connected in this graph.

Why: cables may only change rooms at a door or a passage, and a zone must be joined the same way.

List every legal zone and prove the smallest number of hubs

Every connected group of rooms that fits within 100 m² and 5 devices is a candidate zone. From the area and the number of rooms, 8ball also works out the fewest hubs that any answer could possibly use.

Connected-subgraph enumeration (ESU)Lower bound

  • Connected groups are listed without repeats using the ESU algorithm for enumerating connected subgraphs.
  • Lower bound on hubs = the larger of ⌈total area ÷ 100⌉ and ⌈number of rooms ÷ 5⌉. Nothing can beat it.
  • An exact search then looks for a split of all rooms into that many zones. If none exists (for example because of how the doors lie), it tries one more hub and records why.

Draw a routing map and search it

To find the cheapest cable path with only right-angle turns, 8ball draws a grid of lines through every wall corner, door, passage and device. The best right-angle path always runs along such lines. It then searches the grid for the cheapest path.

Hanan gridA* searchDistance fields

  • The search is A*, which explores the cheapest options first and uses a smart guess of the distance left. Its state includes the direction the cable is heading, because every turn costs 0.5.
  • Every grid edge is tagged with the room it lies in, so the search can refuse rooms a zone may not use.
  • Parallel cables need their own lines: extra lanes 3 cm apart are added so two cables never share a line by accident.
  • From each device, "distance fields" store the cost to reach every grid point in each heading. They are reused for hub placement in step 6.

Build the best loop for each zone

A zone with 5 devices can be visited in at most 12 different orders. 8ball tries every order, routes the cable for each, and keeps the cheapest loops that obey the rules.

Exhaustive cyclesDynamic programmingLane routing

  • For each order, a dynamic-programming pass chooses the direction in which the cable leaves and enters each device, so the turn at every device is counted.
  • Legs are routed one after another. Each new leg keeps off the lines of the earlier legs (using the lanes) and pays 5 if it has to touch their cable.
  • The true cost J of each finished loop is calculated with the real scoring rules, not an estimate.
  • A loop never runs back along its own cable. That keeps overlap at zero and avoids a rule dispute over what counts as a dead-end room.

Place the hub

The hub goes where it adds the least extra cable. For every possible spot, 8ball works out how much longer the loop gets if it detours through that spot. A spot that already lies on a straight stretch of cable costs nothing. These numbers are the heatmap you see in the viewer.

Insertion costForward and reverse fields

  • For a spot and a leg (device A to device B), the extra cost = cheapest way A to the spot + any turn at the spot + cheapest way from the spot to B, minus the cost of A to B. The cheapest leg and headings win.
  • Spots are limited to places at least 0.5 m from walls and 0.3 m from devices, inside the zone's own rooms.
  • The heatmap shows this cost for a fine lattice of spots. The chosen hub is the cheapest one.

Pick the best set of zones

Now 8ball chooses which zones to use so that every room is covered exactly once, with the fewest hubs and then the lowest J. Loops of different zones can cross each other, so it checks the real cost of the best combinations.

Exact cover searchClash tableBeam searchCP-SAT

  • Every way to cover all rooms with the fewest zones is tried. For each, the best loop of each zone is chosen using a table of how much each pair of loops clashes (crossings and overlap).
  • If there are too many combinations, a beam search keeps only the most promising ones at each step.
  • A second method solves the same choice: CP-SAT, a constraint-programming solver, treats it as a set-partitioning problem. It cross-checks the exact search and keeps working on floors far too large for it (see Scaling up).

Polish

8ball takes one leg of a cable out and routes it again in a different way, then puts the hub back in its best spot. It keeps the change only if the real total cost goes down.

Rip-up and rerouteLocal search

  • Three ways to reroute a leg: avoid every other cable, allow touching at a price of 5, or take a different lane.
  • The loop is only accepted if it is still legal (no overlap) and the cost of the whole answer, including clashes with other zones, really drops. The cost never goes up.
  • Finally the cable's corner points are tidied: no repeated points, no needless points on straight stretches, and ends exactly on devices and hubs.

Always have an answer

Before any of the above, a simple greedy solver builds a valid answer. If a search runs out of time, the best answer found so far is returned, so there is never an empty result.

Greedy baselineTime budget

  • The better of the greedy and the exact answer is returned: fewest hubs first, then lowest J. The exact one was never worse in our tests.
  • The live time budget is 30 seconds per plan. Most plans take about two seconds.
  • A plan with no legal way to split its rooms (see the results below) is set aside, with the reason written in the answer.

Check it independently

A second program, written without reading the solver's code, rebuilds the walls and door links itself, checks every rule and recomputes L, X, O and B.

Independent checker1 mm grid

  • It checks the answer's format and six groups of rules: coverage (every room in exactly one zone, every device served once), capacity, zone connectivity, the ring itself, free space (walls, doors, other zones) and the hub spot.
  • It measures everything on a 1 mm grid. If its numbers differ from the solver's by even a millimetre, that is counted as a bug.

Why: if two separate implementations agree on thousands of plans, the numbers can be trusted.

Scaling up

A flat is small. A real building can have dozens or hundreds of rooms, and the number of possible zone choices explodes as rooms are added, so a method that is instant for one flat can become hopeless for a tower. To test this we glue several real flats into one large floor and time how long each method takes to choose the zones.
